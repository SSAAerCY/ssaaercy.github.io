The “COM ice fingerprint range” contains weak absorption features that can reveal which molecules are stored in protostellar ices. Identifying their carriers is difficult, however, because the bands are faint and often overlap. In my 2026 study, I measured four bands at $\mathdefault{7.02}$, $\mathdefault{7.24}$, $\mathdefault{7.40}$, and $\mathdefault{7.67\,\mu m}$ in JWST/MIRI-MRS spectra of 21 protostars, extending earlier studies of individual sources to a larger sample.

## Measuring weak bands across protostars

I isolated the weak features from the local continuum and, where necessary, removed superposed gas-phase lines before measuring their peak wavelengths, widths, peak optical depths, and integrated areas. Comparing band areas with laboratory ice spectra tests the relative contributions of candidate species, while peak positions and widths help constrain the carriers and their mixing environments independently. I used these measurements to characterize the bands across the sample, without attempting a full laboratory-spectrum decomposition of every source.

The sample was selected to include sources with a clearly detected $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ ice band, providing a reliable reference for the weaker features. The $\mathdefault{7.24}$ and $\mathdefault{7.40\,\mu m}$ bands are recognized together in most sources, although some detections remain tentative at low signal-to-noise ratios. The $\mathdefault{7.02\,\mu m}$ feature is harder to measure because it lies on the wing of the broad $\mathdefault{NH\_{4}^{+}}$ absorption and is more sensitive to the placement of the local continuum.

The isolated spectra are shown in Figure 2 in Chen et al. (2026), vertically offset for clarity and arranged roughly by the strength of the $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ band. Thin and thick traces show the spectra before and after gas-line removal where it was needed, and the dotted lines mark reference wavelengths for the four ice features. Similar patterns recur across many sources, but not all spectra show them equally clearly. Sensitivity must therefore be considered when using detections and nondetections to assess how widespread these ices are.

## From band strengths to molecular carriers

The $\mathdefault{7.24}$ and $\mathdefault{7.40\,\mu m}$ bands appear together in most of the observed Class 0 and Class 0/I protostars, and their integrated areas are strongly correlated. Both observations strongly suggest a contribution from $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ ice, which has two absorption bands near these wavelengths. This shared carrier need not account for all the absorption, so I compared the observations with laboratory spectra to test whether additional species are required.

In the left panel of Figure 3 in Chen et al. (2026), the two weak bands are compared directly with each other and with new laboratory measurements of $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ in different ice mixtures. The middle and right panels compare each weak band with the stronger $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ band at $\mathdefault{7.67\,\mu m}$. All three band areas increase together, suggesting that they trace the ice reservoirs in protostellar envelopes. When ice columns or signal-to-noise ratios are low, the weaker bands may fall below the detection threshold even though their carriers are present.

The tested $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ ice mixtures do not fully reproduce the observed band-area ratios. Their longer-wavelength band is stronger relative to the $\mathdefault{7.24\,\mu m}$ band than it is in the observations. The extra absorption at $\mathdefault{7.24\,\mu m}$ points to additional contributors, such as $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}OH}$, even though the correlation supports $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ as a carrier of both bands.

## What the band profiles reveal

Figure 4 in Chen et al. (2026) compares peak wavelength with full width at half maximum for each of the four bands. The stellar markers represent observations, and the colored laboratory measurements show how a candidate's spectrum changes with ice composition and temperature. A useful identification must match both the position and the width of a feature, rather than its wavelength alone.

The $\mathdefault{7.67\,\mu m}$ band in panel (c) has a particularly tight distribution of peak positions and widths across the sample. This supports a dominant contribution from $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ ice in broadly similar mixing environments. Its profile agrees better with mixed ices than with pure $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$, although more laboratory measurements are needed to constrain the mixtures further.

Panels (a) and (b) strengthen the identification of $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$: its $\mathdefault{H\_{2}O}$-mixed laboratory spectra reproduce the properties of both observed bands better than the pure-ice spectra. Other contributions remain possible. Low-temperature $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}OH}$ mixtures can contribute at $\mathdefault{7.24\,\mu m}$, while some $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$ mixtures may contribute at $\mathdefault{7.40\,\mu m}$. The broader and more scattered $\mathdefault{7.02\,\mu m}$ feature in panel (d) is consistent with contributions from $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$ and potentially $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ in $\mathdefault{H\_{2}O}$-rich mixtures.

## Evidence for widespread chemistry in ices

The correlations and band profiles suggest that weak complex-ice features may be widespread in protostars, even where earlier instruments could not detect them. The presence of the ice salt $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ also indicates chemical activity in the solid phase, including possible acid–base reactions. By comparing the same bands across 21 sources, this study tests molecular identifications independently of the detailed spectral decompositions used for individual objects. Several proposed carriers are supported, but overlapping bands and gaps in laboratory measurements still limit how precisely their contributions can be determined.
