Weak absorption features in the “COM ice fingerprint range” offer a way to investigate the molecular material stored in protostellar ices, but their small amplitudes and overlapping profiles make them difficult to identify. My 2026 study uses JWST/MIRI-MRS spectra of 21 protostars to quantify four bands at $\mathdefault{7.02}$, $\mathdefault{7.24}$, $\mathdefault{7.40}$, and $\mathdefault{7.67\,\mu m}$, extending earlier studies of individual sources to a systematic comparison across a larger sample.

## Measuring weak bands across protostars

I isolated the weak features from the local continuum and, where necessary, removed superposed gas-phase lines before measuring their peak wavelengths, widths, peak optical depths, and integrated areas. These measurements allow two independent comparisons with laboratory ice spectra: band areas test the relative contributions of candidate species, while peak positions and widths constrain their identities and mixing environments. The study characterizes the observed bands rather than deriving a complete molecular inventory through a laboratory-spectrum decomposition for every source.

The sample was selected to include sources with a clearly detected $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ ice band, providing a reliable reference for the weaker features. The $\mathdefault{7.24}$ and $\mathdefault{7.40\,\mu m}$ bands are recognized together in most sources, although some detections remain tentative at low signal-to-noise ratios. The $\mathdefault{7.02\,\mu m}$ feature is harder to measure because it lies on the wing of the broad $\mathdefault{NH\_{4}^{+}}$ absorption and is more sensitive to the placement of the local continuum.

Figure 2 in Chen et al. (2026) shows this comparison across the sample. The spectra are vertically offset for clarity and arranged roughly by the strength of the $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ band. Thin and thick traces show the spectra before and after gas-line removal where it was needed, and the dotted lines mark reference wavelengths for the four ice features. The recurring band patterns are visible across many sources, while the contrast between clear and marginal detections illustrates why sensitivity matters when assessing the prevalence of complex ices.

## From band strengths to molecular carriers

The $\mathdefault{7.24}$ and $\mathdefault{7.40\,\mu m}$ bands appear together in most of the observed Class 0 and Class 0/I protostars, and their integrated areas are strongly correlated. This provides strong evidence for a contribution from $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ ice, which has two absorption bands near these wavelengths. Their co-occurrence and correlated strengths support a shared carrier, while comparisons with laboratory spectra test whether that carrier can account for the full observed absorption.

Figure 3 in Chen et al. (2026) separates two complementary pieces of evidence. The left panel compares the two weak bands directly and includes new laboratory measurements of $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ in different ice mixtures. The middle and right panels compare each weak band with the stronger $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ band at $\mathdefault{7.67\,\mu m}$. All three band areas increase together, suggesting that they are common tracers of ice reservoirs in protostellar envelopes. Weak bands may therefore fall below the detection threshold when ice column densities or signal-to-noise ratios are low, rather than being absent because a source has no such ice reservoir.

The laboratory comparison in the left panel indicates that the tested $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ ice mixtures do not fully reproduce the observed band-area ratios. In the laboratory mixtures, its longer-wavelength band is stronger relative to the $\mathdefault{7.24\,\mu m}$ band than it is in the observations. The excess relative absorption at $\mathdefault{7.24\,\mu m}$ points to additional contributors, such as $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}OH}$. The correlations therefore support $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ ice, while the band-area ratios suggest overlapping contributions from other species.

## What the band profiles reveal

Figure 4 in Chen et al. (2026) compares peak wavelength with full width at half maximum for each of the four bands. The stellar markers represent observations, and the colored laboratory measurements show how a candidate's spectrum changes with ice composition and temperature. A useful identification must match both the position and the width of a feature, rather than its wavelength alone.

The $\mathdefault{7.67\,\mu m}$ band in panel (c) has a particularly tight distribution of peak positions and widths across the sample. This supports a dominant contribution from $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$ ice in broadly similar mixing environments. Its profile agrees better with mixed ices than with pure $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$, although more laboratory measurements are needed to constrain the mixtures further.

Panels (a) and (b) strengthen the identification of $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$: its $\mathdefault{H\_{2}O}$-mixed laboratory spectra reproduce the properties of both observed bands better than the pure-ice spectra. Other contributions remain possible. Low-temperature $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}OH}$ mixtures can contribute at $\mathdefault{7.24\,\mu m}$, while some $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$ mixtures may contribute at $\mathdefault{7.40\,\mu m}$. The broader and more scattered $\mathdefault{7.02\,\mu m}$ feature in panel (d) is consistent with contributions from $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$ and potentially $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ in $\mathdefault{H\_{2}O}$-rich mixtures.

## Evidence for widespread chemistry in ices

Together, the correlations and profile comparisons support the potential ubiquity of weak complex-ice features in protostars and explain why many escaped detection with earlier instruments. The presence of $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ as an ice salt also provides evidence for active solid-phase chemistry, including processes such as acid–base reactions. These statistical constraints complement detailed spectral decompositions of individual objects: they independently support several proposed molecular carriers while showing where blends and incomplete laboratory coverage still limit the interpretation.
