$\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ is among the most abundant three-carbon oxygen-bearing molecules detected in space, yet its formation and evolution remain less explored than those of many smaller organic molecules. My 2025 CoCCoA study combines ALMA observations of 12 high-mass protostars with laboratory results, theoretical calculations, and chemical simulations to investigate its abundance and possible evolution from ice to gas.

## A surprisingly abundant three-carbon molecule

I modeled $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ emission alongside $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}CHO}$, $\mathdefault{CH\_{2}CO}$, and $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CCH}$, which may be linked to its chemistry. $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$, $\mathdefault{CH\_{2}CO}$, and $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CCH}$ are firmly detected throughout the sample. $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}CHO}$ remains tentative because the data contain too few unblended diagnostic lines, so its abundances are treated as upper limits. The excitation and emission morphology place the oxygen-bearing species mainly in hot cores, while $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CCH}$ can trace more extended outflow material.

Figure 5 in Chen et al. (2025) compares the mean column-density ratios relative to $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OH}$ in low- and high-mass protostars. Blue and red bars distinguish the two samples; the hatched regions show their dispersion, and the numbers beneath the bars indicate how many sources contribute. Despite having an extra carbon atom, $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ is not necessarily less abundant than the two-carbon species and can exceed $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$ and $\mathdefault{CH\_{2}OHCHO}$.

The comparison also reveals a broader pattern. Aldehydes are less abundant than several other oxygen-bearing molecules with the same number of carbon atoms, while $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OCH\_{3}}$ and $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OCHO}$ are particularly abundant. This motivates two possibilities that observations alone cannot resolve: some pathways may preferentially form ethers, esters, and ketones, or aldehydes may be destroyed or converted more efficiently after entering the gas.

## Connecting observations to candidate pathways

Figure 7 in Chen et al. (2025) brings together proposed routes from astrochemical simulations, theoretical calculations, and experiments. Solid and dashed arrows distinguish ice and gas reactions, and the different colors identify the source of each proposal. In the MAGICKAL network, one solid-phase route builds $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ through $\mathdefault{CH\_{2}CO}$ and $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$. Other routes involve hydrogen abstraction from radicals related to $\mathdefault{i\text{-}C\_{3}H\_{7}OH}$ or gas-phase reactions of $\mathdefault{2\text{-}C\_{3}H\_{7}}$ with atomic oxygen.

Additional proposals connect $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ to reactions of $\mathdefault{CH\_{2}CO}$ with $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}}$, and to oxygen addition to $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CH{=}CH\_{2}}$ in irradiated ices. These pathways can also produce other isomers, making their relative yields useful tests. However, some theoretical calculations assume conditions unlike interstellar environments, and the relevant reaction efficiencies and branching ratios remain incompletely constrained. The diagram summarizes candidate pathways whose contributions still need to be tested.

## Testing the network with abundance ratios

Figure 8 in Chen et al. (2025) compares $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ with four related species as a function of source luminosity. Within the high-mass CoCCoA sample, its gas-phase ratios relative to $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$, $\mathdefault{CH\_{2}CO}$, and $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CCH}$ generally vary by about an order of magnitude without a clear dependence on luminosity. Many low-mass measurements are comparable, although IRAS 16293 has particularly low ratios relative to $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CHO}$ and $\mathdefault{CH\_{2}CO}$. Differences in the spatial distribution of molecular emission may contribute to these outliers.

MAGICKAL reproduces several of the observed abundance relationships, making the proposed chemical connections plausible. However, agreement in a ratio does not uniquely identify the dominant pathway because different networks can give similar outcomes and errors in two modeled abundances can partly cancel.

Panel (c) exposes an important mismatch. $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ is at least an order of magnitude more abundant than $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}CHO}$ in the gas, whereas the models predict much more comparable amounts. The upward triangles are lower limits on this ratio because $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{5}CHO}$ itself is constrained by upper limits. The discrepancy suggests missing or underestimated destruction pathways for aldehydes, or differences in the formation efficiencies of the two isomers.

Panel (d) uses $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CCH}$ as an indirect probe because it is a hydrogenation precursor of $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CH{=}CH\_{2}}$. Their abundance relationship is suggestive, but direct observations of the proposed precursor and additional products are needed to test that pathway. Their potentially different emitting regions also matter when interpreting the ratios.

## Evolution after sublimation

JWST measurements in the two available low-mass targets suggest that $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}/CH\_{3}OH}$ is about an order of magnitude higher in ice than in typical protostellar gas. If the ice abundances are reliable, this points to processing after sublimation that reduces $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ relative to $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OH}$. The small ice sample and the tentative identification in IRAS 2A remain important limitations.

Together, these comparisons make $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}COCH\_{3}}$ a useful test of how chemical networks build and preserve larger organic molecules. They highlight the need for formation and destruction pathways that can explain both its high gas-phase abundance and the much lower abundance of related aldehydes.
