**ICEBEAR** is a JWST Cycle 4 GO program targeting 15 high-mass protostars, with $\mathdefault{44.3\,h}$ of observing time. Together with its ALMA Cycle 12 follow-up program, **2025.1.01411.S**, it investigates how molecular inventories evolve from ice to gas and how the radiation fields around young massive stars excite molecular emission.

## Following molecules from ice to gas with JWST and ALMA

Cold dust-grain ice mantles store a molecular inventory that helps set the chemistry of the warm gas around a protostar. As the surrounding material heats up, molecules sublimate into the gas, where further reactions can alter their abundances. ICEBEAR asks which parts of this ice chemistry are preserved and where molecules are replenished, destroyed, or converted into other species.

JWST/MIRI-MRS measures ice absorption and gas-phase rovibrational features in the mid-infrared, while ALMA detects rotational emission from the gas. Figure 1 in Chen et al. (2024) illustrates how these observations connect to the growth and evolution of ice mantles: ALMA traces gas in the hot core, whereas JWST absorption samples ice and gas along the line of sight through the envelope. Interpreting their abundance comparisons requires accounting for the different regions sampled by each telescope, with chemical models helping to explain the similarities and differences.

## Reading the chemical and physical history of ices

The project uses ice-band profiles and column densities to explore relationships between molecular species and the environments in which they are embedded. Comparisons with laboratory spectra help constrain ice mixtures and the signatures of heating or irradiation. These measurements connect the molecular inventory with the physical evolution of massive protostellar environments.

## An ALMA follow-up to ICEBEAR

The ALMA program **2025.1.01411.S** targets the same 15 high-mass sources to extend the chemistry and excitation studies enabled by JWST. Existing ALMA surveys already provide gas measurements for many complex organic molecules. The follow-up adds observations tailored to simpler molecules, whose rotational transitions are more widely spaced and often require several frequency settings to constrain their abundances and excitation temperatures.

The observations are designed around four spectral setups in ALMA Bands 6 and 7, covering transitions with a range of upper-state energies and minor isotopologues of species whose main lines can be optically thick. Rotational ladders of $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CN}$ provide additional temperature diagnostics. The angular resolution is chosen to be comparable to JWST/MIRI at the wavelengths of the target features, supporting comparisons of the gas seen by the two instruments.

### Extending the gas–ice comparison to simpler molecules

The new ALMA measurements target $\mathdefault{H\_{2}CO}$, $\mathdefault{HNCO}$, and $\mathdefault{HCOOH}$, using $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OH}$ as a common reference for relative abundances in gas and ice. Alongside comparisons of $\mathdefault{H\_{2}CO}$ and $\mathdefault{HCOOH}$ across both phases, the program examines the chemical links between $\mathdefault{OCN^{-}}$ ice and $\mathdefault{HNCO}$ gas, and between $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ ice and $\mathdefault{HCOOH}$ gas. The ice anions occur in salts with counterions such as $\mathdefault{NH\_{4}^{+}}$, so these comparisons also probe the connection between ionic ice chemistry and neutral gas molecules.

Normalizing the molecular inventories to $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OH}$ allows us to investigate whether their relative abundances are preserved, enhanced, or reduced as material moves into the warm gas. Laboratory ice-band strengths, ALMA spectral modeling, and chemical simulations together provide the basis for interpreting this evolution.

### Testing infrared pumping in warm molecular gas

Infrared radiation can populate molecular vibrational levels, so the strength of a rovibrational emission band depends on both molecular abundance and excitation. Comparing JWST bands with ALMA rotational lines provides a way to assess the contributions of radiation and collisions. The follow-up focuses on $\mathdefault{SO\_{2}}$, $\mathdefault{SiO}$, $\mathdefault{CS}$, and $\mathdefault{HCN}$, whose spectra can be observed with both instruments.

We will combine the JWST and ALMA spectra in non-LTE excitation analyses, supported by the temperature diagnostics and information from JWST $\mathdefault{H\_{2}}$ emission, to test how widespread and significant infrared pumping is in massive protostars. These comparisons can also help assess the assumptions used to interpret infrared spectra of species such as $\mathdefault{CO\_{2}}$, $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$, and $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{2}}$, whose lack of a permanent dipole moment prevents the usual rotational-line measurements with ALMA.

## Science beyond the ice–gas comparison

Beyond the COM ice–gas comparison, ICEBEAR's full mid-infrared spectra offer a broader view of the environments in which massive stars form. Molecular and atomic lines can probe warm gas, shocks, and ionization, while their spatial distributions may help distinguish emission close to the protostars from that along nearby jets and outflow cavities within the observed fields. Stronger ice bands provide opportunities to measure abundant ice species, such as $\mathdefault{H\_{2}O}$ and $\mathdefault{CO\_{2}}$, and investigate their thermal processing. Silicate features and the dust continuum can also constrain extinction and dust properties. Together with simultaneous MIRI imaging of the surrounding star-forming regions, these diagnostics can help place the molecular chemistry in its physical context and explore how local conditions influence the observed gas and ice.

The ALMA data will also support studies of hot-core chemistry, molecular emission morphology, and the structures associated with accretion and outflows. Together, the two programs offer complementary ways to connect molecular chemistry with the physical processes shaping massive protostars.
