**ICEBEAR** is a JWST Cycle 4 GO program targeting 15 high-mass protostars, with $\mathdefault{44.3\,h}$ of observing time. I lead the program and its ALMA Cycle 12 follow-up, **2025.1.01411.S**, to study what happens to molecular ices as they enter the warm gas around young massive stars. We will also use the two telescopes to test how infrared radiation excites the gas and affects the molecular emission we observe.

## Following molecules from ice to gas with JWST and ALMA

Ice mantles on cold dust grains store molecules that are released as a protostar heats its surroundings. Some may survive in the warm gas, while others react to form new species or are destroyed. By comparing their abundances in ice and gas, we can examine how much of the ice composition is preserved and where further chemistry may be needed to explain the gas abundances.

JWST/MIRI-MRS measures ice absorption and gas-phase rovibrational features in the mid-infrared, while ALMA detects rotational emission from the gas. The schematic in Figure 1 in Chen et al. (2024) shows the growth of ice mantles and the regions sampled by each telescope. ALMA traces gas in the hot core, whereas JWST absorption samples ice and gas along the line of sight through the envelope. These differences in observing geometry need to be considered before attributing differences in abundance to chemical processing.

## Reading the chemical and physical history of ices

An ice band carries information about both the absorbing molecule and the mixture around it. Its profile can also change as the ice warms or is irradiated. We will compare the observed profiles with laboratory spectra to constrain the ice mixtures and look for evidence of this processing. Measuring the column densities of different species will then let us examine how the ice composition varies between sources and with their physical conditions.

## An ALMA follow-up to ICEBEAR

The ALMA program **2025.1.01411.S** targets the same 15 high-mass sources. Existing ALMA surveys already provide gas measurements for many complex organic molecules, but simpler molecules often have more widely spaced rotational transitions. The follow-up therefore uses several frequency settings to measure the lines needed to constrain their abundances and excitation temperatures.

The observations use four spectral setups in ALMA Bands 6 and 7. They cover transitions with a range of upper-state energies, as well as minor isotopologues of species whose main lines can be optically thick. Rotational ladders of $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}CN}$ provide additional temperature diagnostics. We have chosen an angular resolution comparable to that of JWST/MIRI at the wavelengths of the target features so that the gas measurements can be compared at similar spatial scales.

### Extending the gas–ice comparison to simpler molecules

The new ALMA measurements target $\mathdefault{H\_{2}CO}$, $\mathdefault{HNCO}$, and $\mathdefault{HCOOH}$, using $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OH}$ as a common reference for relative abundances in gas and ice. Alongside comparisons of $\mathdefault{H\_{2}CO}$ and $\mathdefault{HCOOH}$ across both phases, the program examines the chemical links between $\mathdefault{OCN^{-}}$ ice and $\mathdefault{HNCO}$ gas, and between $\mathdefault{HCOO^{-}}$ ice and $\mathdefault{HCOOH}$ gas. The ice anions occur in salts with counterions such as $\mathdefault{NH\_{4}^{+}}$, so these comparisons also probe the connection between ionic ice chemistry and neutral gas molecules.

We will use laboratory ice-band strengths to convert the JWST absorption measurements into ice column densities and fit the ALMA spectra to derive gas column densities. Comparing both sets of measurements relative to $\mathdefault{CH\_{3}OH}$ will show which molecules have similar ratios in ice and gas, and which have higher or lower ratios in the gas. We will then use chemical simulations to test explanations for these differences, including reactions after sublimation.

### Testing infrared pumping in warm molecular gas

Infrared radiation can populate molecular vibrational levels, so a strong rovibrational emission band does not necessarily mean that a molecule is abundant. To separate abundance from excitation, we will compare JWST bands with ALMA rotational lines of $\mathdefault{SO\_{2}}$, $\mathdefault{SiO}$, $\mathdefault{CS}$, and $\mathdefault{HCN}$. These molecules can be observed with both instruments, allowing us to assess the contributions of radiation and collisions to their excitation.

We will fit the JWST and ALMA spectra with non-LTE excitation models, using the temperature diagnostics and information from JWST $\mathdefault{H\_{2}}$ emission to constrain the analysis. The results will test how widespread infrared pumping is in massive protostars and how strongly it affects their spectra. They can also help assess the assumptions used to interpret infrared spectra of $\mathdefault{CO\_{2}}$, $\mathdefault{CH\_{4}}$, and $\mathdefault{C\_{2}H\_{2}}$. These species lack a permanent dipole moment, so the usual rotational-line measurements with ALMA are unavailable.

## Science beyond the ice–gas comparison

The full mid-infrared spectra contain more than the weak bands used to study complex organic ices. Stronger bands of abundant species such as $\mathdefault{H\_{2}O}$ and $\mathdefault{CO\_{2}}$ allow us to measure their ice column densities and look for thermal processing. Silicate features and the dust continuum can constrain extinction and dust properties.

Molecular and atomic emission lines can trace warm gas, shocks, and ionization. Mapping them may help distinguish emission close to the protostars from that along nearby jets and outflow cavities within the observed fields. Simultaneous MIRI imaging covers the surrounding star-forming regions, allowing us to examine where the spectroscopic features occur relative to the larger dust structures.

The ALMA data will also let us study the distribution of molecular emission in the hot cores and structures associated with accretion and outflows. Comparing these maps with the JWST observations will help us examine how local heating, shocks, and other physical conditions affect the gas and ice chemistry.
